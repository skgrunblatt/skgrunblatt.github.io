About Me

I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Physics and Astronomy at the University of Alabama. My research focuses on planetary archaeology and stellar astrophysics, specifically finding and understanding giant planets orbiting giant stars.

Previously, I was an Assistant Research Scientist at Johns Hopkins University (2022–2024), and a Kalbfleisch Postdoctoral Fellow at the American Museum of Natural History / Flatiron Institute (2019–2022). I received my Ph.D. in Astronomy from the Institute for Astronomy at the University of Hawaii in 2019, working with Daniel Huber and Eric Gaidos.

Research Highlights

The giants Pipeline

Developing open-source software for the astronomical community. The giants pipeline is designed to robustly identify and characterize transiting planets around evolved stars, standardizing lightcurve analysis and asteroseismic vetting.

Saunders et al. (JOSS in prep) →

Analyzing Stellar Variability in Planetary Systems

Using a Gaussian Process regression, a nonparametric statistical time-series analysis technique, we can describe and remove the predominant stellar signal to extract planetary signals with greater precision from radial velocity data and photometric lightcurves.

Strafaci, Monroe, and Grunblatt 2026 → Saunders, Grunblatt et al. 2025 → Grunblatt, Saunders et al. 2022 → Grunblatt et al. 2017 → Grunblatt et al. 2015 →

Planet Occurrence Around Evolved Stars

Our search has identified two new planets, K2-97b and K2-132b. Both are gas giants, 1.3 times the size and half the mass of Jupiter, orbiting their host stars approximately every 9 days. These targets were identified and accurately characterized with asteroseismology.

Grunblatt 2026 (Book Chapter) → Yoshida, Grunblatt & Price-Whelan 2022 → Grunblatt et al. 2019 →

Human Movement Data with hominin.ai

Applying astrophysical time-series analysis tools to human movement data to extract novel insights. This work is supported by NSF SBIR and a2 grants, demonstrating the cross-disciplinary power of astronomical techniques.

Hammerberg, Grunblatt & Kramer 2025 →

Asteroseismology & Galactic Archaeology

By studying the internal structure of stars through their oscillations (asteroseismology), we can trace the history and evolution of the Milky Way. This intersection of stellar astrophysics and galactic archaeology helps us precisely characterize exoplanet host stars and understand the timeline of planet formation across different galactic environments.

Lindsay et al. 2025 → Nnadi & Grunblatt 2023 → Grunblatt et al. 2021 →

Planet Re-Inflation

K2-97b and K2-132b are likely the first known planets to be currently re-inflated. Their incident flux history indicates they were too cool to inflate until their host stars became red giants, providing evidence that planets may be inflated directly by incident stellar radiation.

Saunders, Grunblatt et al. 2025 → Saunders, Grunblatt et al. 2024 → Pereira, Grunblatt, Saunders et al. 2024 → Grunblatt, Saunders et al. 2022 → Saunders, Grunblatt et al. 2022 →

Transit Timing Analyses

Our transit timing analyses provide crucial insights into the dynamics of exoplanetary systems. By precisely measuring the transit times of known planets, we can identify transit timing variations (TTVs) indicative of complex orbital architectures and ongoing orbital decay.

Sodickson & Grunblatt 2025 →

Late Stage Atmospheric Evolution

Our research explores the balance between planetary reinflation and atmospheric mass loss at late evolutionary stages. By characterizing evolved hot Neptunes orbiting giant stars, we can better understand how intense stellar irradiation affects these smaller planets as their host stars age.

Grunblatt, Saunders et al. 2024 →

Transiting Planets in Distant Environments

Exploring the potential of the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope to discover and characterize transiting exoplanets in distant galactic environments, providing crucial insights into planet formation across the Milky Way.

Grunblatt et al. 2023 →

Eccentric Giants Orbiting Giants

Analysis of radial velocity measurements from the Keck-I telescope revealed that both planets discovered by our survey were on moderately eccentric orbits, suggesting migration from longer period, more eccentric orbits.

Grunblatt, Saunders et al. 2023 → Grunblatt et al. 2018 →
